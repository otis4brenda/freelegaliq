Will vs. Trust: Which One Do You Actually Need?

The real differences between wills and trusts, what each costs, and how to decide which is right for your situation.

📅 June 2026  ·  8 min read  ·  Estate Planning
Written and researched by FreeLegalIQ Editorial Team  ·  Methodology & Sources

You know you should have estate planning documents. But when you start researching, you quickly run into the will vs. trust question — and most explanations either oversimplify or drown you in legal jargon. Here's the plain-English version.

Spoiler: most people with any real assets benefit from a trust. But whether you need one right now depends on your situation. Use our estate planning cost calculator to estimate what a proper plan would cost for your specific situation.

What a Will Actually Does

A last will and testament is a document that directs how you want your assets distributed after death. It can also name a guardian for your minor children, which is reason alone to have one if you're a parent.

Here's the critical thing most people don't know: a will does not avoid probate. Probate is the court-supervised process of validating your will and distributing your estate. In most states it:

  • Takes 6–18 months to complete
  • Costs 3–8% of estate value in attorney and court fees
  • Is a public record — anyone can look up what you owned and who got it
  • Delays distribution to your beneficiaries during the process

For a $500,000 estate, probate could cost $15,000–$40,000 in fees and a year of waiting. A will gets your wishes on record, but doesn't protect your family from this process.

What a Revocable Living Trust Does Differently

A revocable living trust is a legal entity you create while alive that holds your assets. You're typically both the trustee (managing it) and the beneficiary (using it) during your lifetime. When you die, a successor trustee you named distributes assets directly to your beneficiaries — no probate required.

💡 The main advantages of a trust over a will: avoids probate (saves time and money), stays private, can manage your assets if you become incapacitated, and allows more nuanced instructions (staggered distributions to young heirs, conditions, etc.).

A trust also handles incapacity during your lifetime. If you become unable to manage your finances due to illness or accident, your successor trustee steps in immediately — no court conservatorship needed.

Cost Comparison: Will vs. Trust

Upfront costs favor the will, but long-term the trust often wins:

  • Simple will (attorney): $300–$1,000
  • Simple will (online service): $100–$200
  • Revocable living trust package (attorney): $1,500–$3,500 (includes trust, pour-over will, healthcare directive, power of attorney)
  • Trust package (online service like Trust & Will): $300–$600
  • Cost of probate your will forces: 3–8% of estate value

On a $400,000 estate, even a modest 4% probate cost is $16,000. A $2,000 trust setup that avoids that is a clear win. That math shifts for smaller estates or in states with simplified probate procedures.

📊 Example: $350,000 Estate — Will vs. Trust

Will route: Attorney drafts will ($750) + probate attorney fees after death ($10,500) + court costs ($3,000) + 14 months wait = ~$14,250 total cost

Trust route: Attorney drafts trust package ($2,200) + transfer assets + no probate = ~$2,200 total cost. Savings: ~$12,000 and over a year of delays.

Who Should Have a Will (At Minimum)

Everyone over 18 should have at least a basic will. It's especially critical if you:

  • Have minor children who need a named guardian
  • Have strong feelings about who should or should not receive your assets
  • Own any property at all — even a car or small bank account

If you die without a will (called dying "intestate"), state law decides who gets what. That often means assets go to a spouse or children in rigid proportions, which may not match your wishes. In some states, a long-term unmarried partner gets nothing.

Who Should Have a Trust

A revocable living trust makes sense if you:

  • Own real estate, especially in multiple states (each state requires separate probate)
  • Have an estate worth more than $100,000–$150,000
  • Want to keep your estate out of the public record
  • Have beneficiaries you want to protect (children with special needs, young adults who need staggered distributions)
  • Are older or have health concerns (incapacity planning is a huge benefit)
  • Live in a state with expensive or slow probate (California, New York, Florida)

⚠️ A trust only works if you "fund" it — meaning you actually transfer your assets into the trust. An unfunded trust is worthless. Make sure any real estate deeds, bank accounts, and investment accounts are retitled in the name of the trust after signing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a will and a trust?

A will is a legal document that directs how your assets are distributed after death — but it must go through probate, a court-supervised process that takes months and costs money. A revocable living trust holds your assets during your lifetime and transfers them directly to beneficiaries at death without probate, providing privacy, speed, and often lower overall cost. Unlike a will, a trust can also manage your assets if you become incapacitated.

How much does a will vs. a trust cost?

A simple will drafted by an attorney costs $300–$1,000. An online will service costs $100–$200. A revocable living trust package (including the trust document, pour-over will, healthcare directive, and power of attorney) typically costs $1,500–$3,500 with an attorney, or $300–$600 with an online service like Trust & Will or Willing. However, trusts save probate costs later — probate can cost 3–8% of estate value in attorney and court fees.

When do you need both a will and a trust?

Most people who create a trust also need a "pour-over will" — a short will that catches any assets not transferred into the trust during your lifetime and pours them into the trust at death. So if you have a trust, you'll typically have a will too. You might also need a separate will to name a guardian for minor children, since trusts cannot name guardians. Having both documents together gives you complete coverage.

When a Will Is the Right Choice for You

A will is the right — and sometimes only — estate planning tool for many people. Here's when a will alone makes sense:

You have a relatively small estate. If your estate is below your state's simplified probate threshold (typically $50,000–$100,000), probate is fast and inexpensive anyway. The cost of setting up and maintaining a trust often outweighs the savings. In many states, estates under $100,000 can be distributed through a simple affidavit without any court involvement at all.

You have minor children and need to name a guardian. This is actually the most critical reason many young parents need a will — trusts cannot name guardians. A will is the legal mechanism for designating who raises your children if you die. Even people who create trusts should have a companion will for this reason alone.

Your assets pass outside probate anyway. Life insurance, retirement accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s), and accounts with payable-on-death (POD) or transfer-on-death (TOD) designations all pass directly to named beneficiaries without probate — no will or trust required. If most of your wealth is in these vehicles, a simple will may be all the additional coverage you need.

You're young, healthy, and just starting out. A will is a sensible first step that takes an hour to create online for under $200. You can upgrade to a full trust package later when your estate is larger or more complex. Having something in place is vastly better than having nothing — roughly 68% of American adults die without any estate plan at all.

When a Trust Is Worth the Extra Cost

A revocable living trust requires more upfront effort and cost, but the payoff is significant in the right circumstances.

You own real estate. Real property must go through probate in every state where it's located. If you own a vacation home in a different state from where you live, your heirs could face probate in two states simultaneously — called ancillary probate. A trust eliminates this entirely, because the trust owns the property, not you personally. This alone justifies the cost of a trust for many homeowners.

You live in California, Florida, or New York. As the table above shows, probate in statutory fee states can cost $26,000–$40,000 on a $500,000 estate. A trust package costs $1,500–$3,500 from an attorney. The math is obvious: in these states, a trust pays for itself many times over.

You want privacy. Wills become public record when admitted to probate — anyone can look up your will, the size of your estate, and who received what. Trusts are completely private. If privacy matters to you (it does to most people once they think about it), a trust is the only instrument that provides it.

You're concerned about incapacity. A will only operates at death. But a revocable living trust operates during your lifetime — if you become incapacitated due to illness, stroke, or accident, your named successor trustee can step in immediately to manage your assets without any court intervention. This is a significant advantage over a durable power of attorney, which banks and institutions sometimes refuse to honor.

Estate Planning for Digital Assets in 2026

One area where both wills and trusts have traditionally fallen short is digital assets — and this gap is growing as more of our wealth and important accounts move online. In 2026, digital estate planning requires specific attention.

Digital assets that may need planning include: cryptocurrency (Bitcoin, Ethereum, and others), online brokerage accounts, PayPal and Venmo balances, domain names and websites, digital art and NFTs, social media accounts, cloud-stored photos and documents, and subscription services with accumulated value. Without explicit instructions, most of these assets are inaccessible or lost entirely after death.

Most states have adopted the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (RUFADAA), which gives executors and trustees legal authority to access digital assets — but only if your estate planning documents explicitly grant that authority. A generic will or trust drafted before this became standard may not include the right language.

Best practices for digital estate planning in 2026: Create a secure digital inventory document listing your important accounts, usernames, and instructions (not passwords) for accessing them. Store this in a secure password manager or encrypted file and tell your executor or successor trustee where to find it. Consider using a service like Google's Inactive Account Manager or Apple's Legacy Contact feature. Make sure your estate planning documents include explicit language authorizing your fiduciary to access digital assets. Update your documents as your digital life changes — a trust or will drafted 10 years ago almost certainly doesn't address cryptocurrency or current digital platforms adequately.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can I write my own will without a lawyer?

Yes — a handwritten (holographic) will is legally valid in about 25 states, and typed wills signed before two witnesses are valid in all states. Online will services like Trust & Will, Willing, and LegalZoom make this easy for $100–$200. However, DIY wills carry real risks: unclear language, missed assets, improper execution, or failure to account for state-specific rules can invalidate a will or create family disputes. For complex situations — second marriages, blended families, minor children, business interests, or large estates — an estate planning attorney is worth the cost.

Q: What happens if I die without a will or trust?

Dying without a will is called dying "intestate." Your state's intestacy laws then determine who inherits your assets — typically your spouse, then children, then other relatives in a fixed order defined by statute. This often produces results you wouldn't have chosen: a live-in partner receives nothing, a child you're estranged from inherits equally with others, or a distant relative you've never met ends up with your property. Your estate still goes through probate, but without your guidance — the court appoints an administrator and follows the statutory formula. If you have minor children and no surviving spouse, the court also chooses their guardian.

Q: Does a living trust protect assets from creditors?

A revocable living trust does NOT protect assets from your creditors during your lifetime. Because you retain control and can revoke the trust at any time, the law treats the trust assets as your own — creditors can still reach them. Probate avoidance and incapacity planning are the main benefits of a revocable trust, not asset protection. If asset protection is your goal, you need an irrevocable trust, a domestic asset protection trust (DAPT), or other specialized structures — which require an estate planning attorney and forfeit your control over the assets.

Q: How do I fund a living trust?

Creating a trust document is only half the job — you must also "fund" the trust by transferring your assets into it. For real estate, this means recording a new deed that transfers title from your name to the trust. For financial accounts, you contact your bank or brokerage and request a change of ownership to the trust. Vehicles are retitled at your DMV. Life insurance and retirement accounts are not transferred into the trust — instead, you can name the trust as a beneficiary if desired. Many people create a trust and then never fund it, which is a costly mistake — an unfunded trust provides no probate avoidance benefit.

Q: What is a pour-over will, and do I need one?

A pour-over will is a companion document to a living trust that captures any assets you didn't transfer into the trust during your lifetime. When you die, these "leftover" assets go through probate, and then the pour-over will directs them into your trust for distribution. Every person with a living trust should also have a pour-over will. It acts as a safety net for assets acquired near death, accounts you forgot to retitle, or property that can't easily be transferred to a trust during your lifetime. Without it, assets outside your trust may be distributed under intestacy laws rather than your wishes.

Q: How often should I update my will or trust?

Estate planning documents should be reviewed at least every three to five years and updated after any major life event: marriage, divorce, birth or adoption of a child, death of a named beneficiary or executor, significant change in assets, moving to a different state, or a major change in tax law. State laws on estate planning change periodically, and a document drafted 10+ years ago may not reflect current law or your current wishes. At minimum, review your beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance annually — these override your will or trust and are frequently left stale after life changes.

When a Will Is the Right Choice for You

Despite trusts getting most of the estate planning press, a simple will is genuinely the right — and sufficient — tool for a large segment of the population. You don't always need the complexity and upfront cost of a trust.

A will makes sense if your estate is small. Most states have simplified or summary probate procedures for small estates — typically under $50,000 to $200,000 depending on the state. If your total assets fall below your state's small-estate threshold, probate will be quick, inexpensive, and easy. A will handles it perfectly without the overhead of a trust.

A will is essential if you have minor children. Only a will can name a guardian for your minor children. A trust manages money but cannot designate who raises your kids. Even people who have a trust should also have a will specifically to name a guardian. This single function makes a will non-negotiable for any parent.

A will is sufficient if you live in a low-cost probate state. Wisconsin and Texas probate, for example, is relatively fast and cheap. If you live in one of these states with a modest estate, the cost-benefit analysis of a trust is less compelling. A well-drafted will may be all you need.

A will is the right starting point if you're young and just getting started. Estate planning is better done imperfectly now than perfectly later. If you're 25 with few assets, start with a simple will, a healthcare directive, and a durable power of attorney. You can always upgrade to a trust later as your assets grow.

What a will cannot do: A will cannot avoid probate, cannot provide for incapacity (a separate power of attorney is needed), and becomes a public document after you die. It also does not control assets with beneficiary designations (retirement accounts, life insurance, jointly-titled property) — those pass outside the will regardless of what it says.

When a Trust Is Worth the Extra Cost

A revocable living trust costs significantly more to create than a simple will — typically $1,500–$3,500 with an attorney versus $300–$1,000. But for many people, that upfront investment returns multiple times over in avoided probate costs, time saved, and family stress prevented.

A trust is worth it if you own real estate in multiple states. Each state requires its own probate proceeding for real estate located there. Own a home in California and a vacation property in Florida? Without a trust, your heirs face two separate probate proceedings — in two different states, each with its own attorney fees and timeline. A trust holds real estate across all states and distributes it at death without any probate, period.

A trust is worth it if you live in a high-cost probate state. California's statutory probate fees on a $1 million estate run approximately $46,000 to attorney and executor combined. A trust that costs $3,000 to create saves $43,000. The math is not subtle. If you live in California, Florida, New York, or other high-fee states and own significant assets, a trust is almost always the financially superior choice.

A trust is worth it if privacy matters to you. After probate, your will — including the full inventory of your assets and every beneficiary's name — becomes a public record. Anyone can walk into the probate court and read it. Trusts are private. For business owners, high-net-worth individuals, or anyone who values privacy, this alone can justify the cost.

A trust is worth it for blended families. If you have children from a prior relationship and a current spouse, a trust gives you far more control over how assets are distributed — ensuring your children are protected while also providing for your surviving spouse. A will that goes through probate is easier to contest and harder to enforce precisely.

A trust is worth it if you want to plan for incapacity. A revocable trust allows your successor trustee to step in and manage your affairs if you become incapacitated — without a court-supervised conservatorship. This can save your family the time, cost, and public exposure of a guardianship proceeding.

Estate Planning for Digital Assets in 2026

One of the fastest-growing blind spots in estate planning is digital assets — and most existing wills and trusts were drafted before this became a significant issue. In 2026, the average American has meaningful digital assets that require specific planning to pass to heirs.

What counts as a digital asset: Cryptocurrency and NFTs (potentially significant monetary value), online brokerage accounts, PayPal and Venmo balances, domain names and websites (which can generate ongoing revenue), social media accounts with large followings or monetization, cloud storage containing irreplaceable photos and documents, and subscription services that auto-renew.

The access problem: The biggest challenge with digital assets isn't legal — it's practical. Your heirs need your passwords, two-factor authentication codes, and often account recovery information to access anything. Without this, accounts are locked and assets are effectively lost. Cryptocurrency held in a personal wallet without the private key is simply gone forever; no court order can recover it.

How to plan for digital assets: Start by creating a comprehensive digital asset inventory — every account, its approximate value, and where credentials are stored. Never put passwords in a will (it becomes a public document). Instead, use a password manager like 1Password or Bitwarden and give your successor trustee or executor instructions for accessing it. Store the master password in a sealed envelope with your attorney, in a safe, or using a dead-man's switch service. Update your will or trust to specifically authorize your executor/trustee to access, manage, and distribute digital assets under the federal RUFADAA (Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act), which most states have adopted.

Cryptocurrency specifically: Hardware wallets (Ledger, Trezor) should have their seed phrases documented and stored securely — not digitally. A seed phrase written on paper and stored in a fireproof safe, with instructions for how to use it, is the most reliable inheritance mechanism for crypto. Consider a multi-signature wallet setup for larger holdings that requires trustee authorization to move funds.

Social media and online accounts: Facebook, Google, and Apple all have legacy contact or inactive account manager features that let you designate someone to manage or download your account data after death. Set these up proactively — they don't require a lawyer and take about 10 minutes.

Frequently Asked Questions About Wills and Trusts

Q: Does a trust avoid all taxes?

A revocable living trust does not avoid estate taxes. Because you retain full control of the trust during your lifetime, the IRS treats trust assets as part of your taxable estate. The federal estate tax only applies to estates over $12.92 million per individual in 2026 (subject to change if the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act provisions sunset). Irrevocable trusts — which involve giving up control — can be used for estate tax planning, but they are complex, expensive tools appropriate only for high-net-worth individuals. A revocable trust avoids probate costs and delays, not taxes.

Q: Can I write my own will without an attorney?

Yes — in most states, a handwritten (holographic) will is valid if it's entirely in your handwriting and signed. Typed wills generally require two witnesses and sometimes a notary. Online services like Trust & Will, Willing, and LegalZoom provide legally valid will templates for $100–$200. These are appropriate for simple estates with clear asset distribution, no complex family dynamics, and no business interests. Complex situations — blended families, business ownership, special-needs beneficiaries, or significant assets — warrant an attorney. An improperly executed will can be invalidated, leaving your estate in intestacy (state default distribution rules).

Q: What happens if I die without a will or trust?

Dying without a will is called dying "intestate." Your state's intestacy laws determine who inherits your assets — typically a spouse, then children, then parents, then siblings. This may not match your wishes. Unmarried partners receive nothing under intestacy laws in most states regardless of how long the relationship lasted. Stepchildren are typically excluded unless legally adopted. Your estate still goes through probate, and the court appoints an administrator (who may or may not be who you'd choose) to manage the process. Having even a basic will eliminates this uncertainty.

Q: How often should I update my will or trust?

Review your estate plan whenever a major life event occurs: marriage, divorce, birth of a child, death of a beneficiary or executor, significant change in assets, moving to a different state, or change in tax laws. As a baseline, estate planning attorneys recommend reviewing your documents every 3–5 years even without a triggering event. Outdated beneficiary designations — particularly on retirement accounts and life insurance, which pass outside the will — are one of the most common and costly estate planning mistakes. A 10-minute annual beneficiary review can prevent years of legal disputes.

Q: Can a trust be contested in court?

Yes, trusts can be contested, though they are generally harder to challenge than wills. Common grounds for contesting a trust include lack of mental capacity at the time of signing, undue influence by a beneficiary or caregiver, fraud, or improper execution. Because trusts are private documents (unlike wills, which become public in probate), fewer people know their contents, and potential challengers often have less information to work with. A properly drafted, notarized trust with contemporaneous documentation of the grantor's mental capacity is the best defense against a successful challenge.

Q: Do retirement accounts and life insurance go through probate?

No — assets with named beneficiaries (retirement accounts like IRAs and 401(k)s, life insurance policies, annuities, and jointly-titled bank accounts) pass directly to the named beneficiary without going through probate, regardless of what your will says. This is both a major advantage (speed, privacy, no court involvement) and a common trap — many people forget to update beneficiary designations after divorce, remarriage, or the death of a named beneficiary. An outdated beneficiary designation on a $500,000 IRA overrides even the clearest will. Review all beneficiary designations annually.
