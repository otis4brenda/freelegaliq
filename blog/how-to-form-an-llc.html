How to Form an LLC: Step-by-Step Guide + What It Actually Costs

A plain-English walkthrough of every step to form your LLC — plus real cost numbers by state.

📅 June 2026  ·  8 min read  ·  Business Law
Written and researched by FreeLegalIQ Editorial Team  ·  Methodology & Sources

Forming an LLC (Limited Liability Company) is one of the best moves a small business owner or freelancer can make. It separates your personal assets from business liabilities, gives you credibility, and offers tax flexibility. The good news: it's not that complicated, and it's not that expensive.

Here's a complete step-by-step guide to forming an LLC, plus use our LLC formation cost calculator to get exact costs for your state.

Why Form an LLC?

Before the how, here's the why. An LLC gives you:

  • Personal liability protection: If your business is sued or can't pay debts, your personal bank account, home, and savings are protected (as long as you maintain proper separation)
  • Tax flexibility: By default, a single-member LLC is taxed as a sole proprietor (pass-through) and a multi-member LLC as a partnership. You can also elect S-Corp taxation to save on self-employment taxes once income is high enough
  • Credibility: "XYZ Consulting LLC" looks more established than operating under your personal name
  • Easier banking and contracts: Business bank accounts, contracts, and client relationships are cleaner under a business entity

💡 You don't need an LLC to start a business — but once you have clients, employees, or any real revenue, the liability protection is worth the small cost of formation.

Step 1: Choose Your State

Most small businesses should form their LLC in the state where they actually operate. You'll need to register there anyway as a "foreign LLC" if you form elsewhere, which doubles your costs and paperwork.

Exception: Delaware, Wyoming, and Nevada are popular for privacy and asset protection reasons for larger operations. But for a typical small business, just use your home state.

Step 2: Choose Your LLC Name

Your LLC name must:

  • Be unique in your state (check your Secretary of State's name database)
  • Include "LLC," "L.L.C.," or "Limited Liability Company" in the name
  • Not include restricted words without approval (like "Bank," "Insurance," or "University")

Check domain availability at the same time — you'll want a matching domain name for your business website.

Step 3: File Articles of Organization

This is the core legal filing that creates your LLC. You file it with your state's Secretary of State (or equivalent) along with the filing fee. Most states let you file online.

What the Articles of Organization typically include:

  • Your LLC name and address
  • Your registered agent's name and address
  • Member/manager information (varies by state)
  • Purpose of the business

State filing fees range from $50 (Kentucky, Colorado) to $500 (Massachusetts, Illinois). Most states charge $100–$200. Check your exact state cost with our LLC cost calculator.

Step 4: Appoint a Registered Agent

Every LLC must have a registered agent — a person or company with a physical address in your state who can receive legal documents on your behalf during business hours. You have three options:

  • Be your own registered agent: Free, but your home address becomes public record and you must be available during business hours
  • Use a registered agent service: $50–$150/year. Services like Northwest Registered Agent or Registered Agent Inc. provide privacy and reliability
  • Have an attorney serve as agent: Typically included if an attorney handles your formation

Step 5: Create an Operating Agreement

An Operating Agreement is an internal document that governs how your LLC operates. Some states require it; all should have one. It covers:

  • Ownership percentages (for multi-member LLCs)
  • How profits and losses are distributed
  • Voting rights and decision-making
  • What happens if a member wants to leave or dies
  • Management structure (member-managed vs. manager-managed)

⚠️ Without an Operating Agreement, your LLC's operations are governed by default state rules, which may not reflect your intentions. For single-member LLCs, it also reinforces the separation between you and your business — critical for liability protection.

Step 6: Get Your EIN and Open a Business Bank Account

An Employer Identification Number (EIN) is your business's tax ID, like a Social Security Number for your LLC. Apply for free on the IRS website — you get it instantly online. You need an EIN to:

  • Open a business bank account
  • Hire employees
  • File business taxes
  • Apply for business credit

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to form an LLC?

LLC formation costs vary by state. The state filing fee (Articles of Organization) ranges from $50 (Kentucky, Colorado) to $500 (Massachusetts). Most states charge $100–$200. If you use an online formation service like Northwest Registered Agent or ZenBusiness, add $0–$150 for their service. You'll also need a registered agent ($50–$150/year) and an Operating Agreement (free to $200). Total first-year cost is typically $150–$800 depending on your state.

How long does it take to form an LLC?

Standard LLC formation takes 1–3 weeks in most states after filing Articles of Organization. Many states offer expedited processing for an additional fee — same-day or next-business-day approval for $50–$200 extra. Delaware and Wyoming are known for fast, straightforward formation. After formation, getting an EIN from the IRS is instant online. A business bank account can be opened the same day you have your EIN and formation documents.

Do I need a lawyer to form an LLC?

You do not need a lawyer to form a basic single-member or simple multi-member LLC. The formation process is straightforward enough that many owners handle it themselves or use an online formation service. However, an attorney is worth consulting if you have multiple members with different ownership stakes, complex operating agreement terms, or are forming an LLC for a high-liability business. A business attorney can also ensure your Operating Agreement protects you properly.

Which State Should You Form Your LLC In?

The most common question first-time LLC founders ask is whether they should form in their home state or in a "tax-friendly" state like Delaware, Wyoming, or Nevada. The answer depends on where you actually operate your business — and the answer is almost always simpler than the internet makes it seem.

Form in your home state if you are a solo founder, freelancer, or small business with a single location. When you operate in a state, you must register there (either as a domestic or foreign LLC) and pay that state's taxes regardless of where you originally formed. If you form in Delaware but operate in Texas, you'll pay Delaware fees and Texas foreign registration fees — costing more, not less. For most small businesses, the home state is the right state.

Delaware is the gold standard for venture-backed startups and companies planning to raise institutional funding. Delaware's Court of Chancery is the most sophisticated business court in the country, providing legal certainty that investors and their attorneys expect. Delaware has no income tax on out-of-state income, strong LLC privacy protections, and a well-established body of corporate law. However, Delaware charges a $110 filing fee plus $400/year in franchise tax — costs that only make sense if you need the legal infrastructure Delaware provides.

Wyoming has emerged as the top choice for privacy-focused founders and those who want a low-cost alternative to Delaware. Wyoming charges $100 to form and $60/year minimum to maintain, has no state income tax, and allows nominee officers and members — meaning your name doesn't have to appear in public records. Wyoming also has strong charging order protections that limit a creditor's ability to seize your LLC interest. For online businesses, consultants, and asset-protection-focused owners, Wyoming is genuinely compelling.

Nevada markets itself aggressively as a business-friendly state, but at $425 to form and $350/year in fees, the costs are high compared to Wyoming, which offers many of the same protections at a fraction of the price. Nevada's privacy and asset protection benefits are real, but Wyoming often delivers them for less. Unless you have a specific reason to be in Nevada — like operating a business there — Wyoming is typically the better remote-formation choice in 2026.

Bottom line: If you're a one-person business or small team operating locally, form in your home state. If you need investor-ready legal infrastructure, choose Delaware. If you want maximum privacy and asset protection as an online or multi-state business, Wyoming offers the best value.

Common LLC Formation Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

Even straightforward LLC formations go wrong when founders skip steps or misunderstand requirements. Here are the most common mistakes — and exactly how to avoid them.

1. Skipping the Operating Agreement. Most states don't legally require an operating agreement for single-member LLCs, but skipping it is a costly mistake. Without one, your LLC's internal rules are governed by default state law — which may not reflect how you actually want your business to run. Worse, courts have occasionally pierced the corporate veil of LLCs without operating agreements because they found the LLC indistinguishable from the owner personally. A simple operating agreement takes an hour to draft and costs nothing if you use a free template.

2. Mixing personal and business finances. The primary legal benefit of an LLC — limited liability protection — only holds if you treat your LLC as a separate entity. That means a dedicated business checking account, separate credit cards, and no personal expenses run through the business account. Commingling funds is the single fastest way to lose your liability protection in court.

3. Missing annual report deadlines. Most states require annual or biennial reports to keep your LLC in good standing. Missing these deadlines results in late fees ($50–$500), and eventually the state can administratively dissolve your LLC. Set calendar reminders for your state's filing deadline every year.

4. Choosing the wrong registered agent. Many founders list themselves as their own registered agent to save money, but this means your name and address are in the public record and you must be physically available at that address during business hours to receive legal documents. If you ever need to take a vacation or move, this creates compliance headaches. Professional registered agent services cost $50–$150/year and are often worth it for the privacy and reliability they provide.

5. Not getting an EIN when you need one. Even if you're a single-member LLC with no employees, you'll need an EIN to open a business bank account, apply for business credit, and file certain tax elections. The IRS provides EINs instantly online at no charge — there is never any reason to pay a third-party service for this.

6. Forgetting foreign qualification. If you form your LLC in Wyoming but operate a physical business in Arizona, you must register as a foreign LLC in Arizona too. Operating without foreign qualification can result in fines, back taxes, and an inability to sue in state courts. Always register in every state where you have significant business presence.

Understanding Registered Agents: What They Do and Why You Need One

Every LLC in every state is required to maintain a registered agent — also called a statutory agent or agent for service of process. Understanding this role is essential for every business owner.

What a registered agent does: The registered agent is the official point of contact between your LLC and the state government. They receive service of process (legal documents like lawsuits and subpoenas), annual report reminders, tax notices, and other official government correspondence on behalf of your business. When someone files a lawsuit against your company, the plaintiff's attorney serves the complaint to your registered agent — not directly to you.

Requirements: Your registered agent must have a physical street address (not a P.O. box) in the state of formation, and must be available during normal business hours. The agent can be you personally, any adult member of your LLC, an attorney, or a professional registered agent service.

Why use a professional service: Professional registered agents ($50–$150/year from services like Northwest Registered Agent, ZenBusiness, or Incfile) provide several advantages. First, they keep your personal address out of the public record — important if you work from home. Second, they provide consistent availability regardless of your schedule or travel. Third, they offer document scanning and forwarding, ensuring you never miss a critical legal deadline because a document sat unnoticed in a mailbox.

Consequences of failing: If your registered agent is unavailable when served with legal process, a court can enter a default judgment against your LLC — potentially for the full amount the plaintiff is seeking. If your registered agent address is invalid, the state may lose the ability to contact your LLC, which can lead to administrative dissolution. Maintaining a reliable, available registered agent is one of the lowest-cost and highest-impact compliance steps you can take.

Frequently Asked Questions About LLC Formation

Q: Can I form an LLC in a different state than where I live?

Yes, you can form an LLC in any state regardless of where you live. However, if your business operates physically in your home state — you have an office, employees, or sell goods there — you'll need to register as a foreign LLC in your home state anyway and pay that state's fees. For most local businesses, forming at home is simpler and cheaper. Remote-operation businesses (like online stores or consulting practices) can benefit from forming in Wyoming or Delaware without foreign qualification complications.

Q: How long does LLC formation take?

Standard processing takes 1–3 weeks in most states after you file Articles of Organization. Many states offer expedited processing for an additional $50–$200 — some (like Delaware and Wyoming) can approve same-day or next business day. After approval, getting an EIN from the IRS is instant online, and you can open a business bank account the same day you have your EIN and formation documents. Total time from decision to fully operational: 1–4 weeks for most founders.

Q: Is an LLC better than a sole proprietorship?

For most businesses with any meaningful revenue or liability risk, yes. A sole proprietorship offers zero personal liability protection — your personal assets (home, savings, car) are fully exposed if your business is sued or defaults on a debt. An LLC creates a legal separation between you and your business. The cost is minimal ($35–$500 to form) compared to the protection it provides. The exception is very new businesses testing a concept with minimal risk — a sole proprietorship is fine for the first 90 days before you validate the idea.

Q: What taxes does an LLC pay?

By default, a single-member LLC is taxed as a disregarded entity — all business income flows to your personal tax return and you pay self-employment tax (15.3%) on net profits. A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership by default. LLCs can elect to be taxed as an S-Corporation (potentially saving self-employment taxes once profits exceed roughly $60,000–$80,000) or as a C-Corporation. The best tax structure depends on your income level, and a CPA can model the difference for your specific situation.

Q: Can a single person own an LLC?

Absolutely. A single-member LLC (SMLLC) is one of the most common business structures in the United States. You can be the sole owner, sole member, and sole manager all at once. Single-member LLCs receive the same liability protection as multi-member LLCs. The primary difference is tax treatment — a single-member LLC is automatically taxed as a disregarded entity unless you elect otherwise, while a multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership by default.

Q: What is the difference between an LLC and a corporation?

Both LLCs and corporations provide limited liability protection, but they differ significantly in structure and taxation. Corporations have a formal structure (board of directors, officers, shareholders, required annual meetings and minutes) that provides regulatory credibility but requires more administrative work. LLCs are more flexible — members can structure management however they want in the operating agreement, and there's no requirement for formal meetings. Corporations are taxed at the corporate level (C-Corp) or pass income through to shareholders (S-Corp), while LLCs can choose their tax treatment. Startups seeking venture capital typically form as C-Corporations (often in Delaware) because investors and their attorneys are most familiar with that structure.
