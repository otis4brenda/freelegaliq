What Happens If You Can't Afford a Lawyer? 7 Options That Actually Help

Legal help doesn't always require a $350/hour attorney. Here are seven real alternatives — from legal aid to law school clinics to strategic self-representation.

📅 August 2026  ·  10 min read  ·  Legal Resources

Attorney fees are genuinely unaffordable for many Americans. At $250–$500 per hour, a contested divorce, a custody modification, a landlord-tenant dispute, or a wrongful termination claim can quickly exceed what most families earn in a month. And unlike criminal cases where the government must provide a public defender, civil legal matters come with no such guarantee.

But "I can't afford a lawyer" doesn't have to mean "I'm on my own." A robust ecosystem of legal resources exists for people who can't pay full freight — from fully funded legal aid organizations to law school clinics staffed by supervised law students to online document services. This guide walks through seven legitimate options, who they're right for, and how to access them.

Option 1: Legal Aid Organizations

Legal aid organizations are nonprofit agencies funded by federal, state, and private grants to provide free civil legal services to low-income individuals. There are over 900 legal aid offices across the country, and they collectively handle millions of cases every year.

Who qualifies: Most legal aid organizations serve people earning below 125–200% of the federal poverty level. For 2026, the federal poverty level is about $15,650 for an individual and $32,150 for a family of four. At 200% FPL, that means roughly $31,300/year for an individual or $64,300 for a family of four can potentially qualify for assistance.

What they handle: Legal aid focuses on civil matters that affect basic human needs — housing (evictions, foreclosures), family law (protective orders, divorce for domestic violence survivors, custody), immigration (asylum, DACA, removal defense), public benefits (Social Security appeals, Medicaid denials), and consumer protection (debt collection, bankruptcy). They do not handle criminal defense (that's the public defender's job) or most business matters.

How to find yours: Go to lawhelp.org and select your state, or call 211 (the social services hotline) and ask for legal aid referrals. You can also search "legal aid" plus your city or county.

📌 Legal aid attorneys are fully licensed professionals — the service is free, not inferior. Many legal aid offices have specialized units with deep expertise in housing law, immigration, or domestic violence cases.

Option 2: Public Defenders (Criminal Cases Only)

If you're facing criminal charges — including DUI — and cannot afford an attorney, you have a constitutional right to a public defender under the Sixth Amendment (Gideon v. Wainwright, 1963). Public defenders are licensed attorneys employed by the government to represent defendants who cannot afford private counsel.

To request a public defender, you typically declare at your first court appearance (arraignment) that you cannot afford private counsel. The court will assess your financial situation — usually based on income and assets — and appoint a public defender if you qualify. Income thresholds vary by jurisdiction.

A realistic note: Public defenders are often overworked, carrying caseloads far exceeding recommended limits. This doesn't mean they're ineffective — many are highly skilled — but it does mean you may get less time with your attorney than you'd like. If at all possible for serious charges, private criminal defense representation typically allows more personalized attention.

Option 3: Pro Bono Attorneys

Many private attorneys take cases for free (pro bono) as a professional obligation or personal commitment. The American Bar Association recommends attorneys devote at least 50 hours per year to pro bono work. In practice, some do much more; others do none.

Pro bono work tends to cluster around causes that generate attorney interest — civil rights, immigration, domestic violence, veterans' legal issues, and nonprofit work. Finding a pro bono attorney often requires a referral from a legal aid organization (which may not be able to take your case but can refer you) or through your state bar's pro bono referral program.

How to find pro bono help:

  • Your state bar association's website — search for "pro bono" program
  • LawHelp.org — filters by case type and income
  • American Bar Association's legal help finder at americanbar.org/legalhelp
  • Law school pro bono clearinghouses
  • Local bar association's lawyer referral service

Option 4: Law School Clinics

Every accredited law school in the United States operates one or more clinical programs where law students handle real cases under the supervision of licensed attorneys (usually professors or experienced supervising attorneys). The service is free to clients, and the work is often excellent — students are highly motivated to succeed on behalf of real clients, and the supervising attorney reviews everything before it's filed.

Law school clinics typically handle a specific area of law — immigration, criminal defense, housing, consumer protection, family law, tax, environmental law, disability rights, or veterans' benefits. Not every clinic is available at every school.

How to find a law school clinic: Search for ABA-accredited law schools near you, then visit the law school's website and look for "clinics" or "experiential learning." Contact the clinic coordinator directly — most have a straightforward intake process.

Option 5: Lawyer Referral Services

Every state bar association operates a lawyer referral service that connects potential clients with attorneys who have agreed to offer an initial consultation at a reduced rate — often $25–$50 for 30 minutes. This is not free legal help, but it is an affordable way to get a licensed attorney's assessment of your case and understand your options before committing to representation.

Some referral services also include attorneys who offer reduced-fee representation for qualifying clients, or who specialize in payment plans. Ask specifically about reduced-fee options when you call.

Option 6: Unbundled Legal Services (Limited Scope Representation)

Unbundled legal services — sometimes called "limited scope representation" or "legal coaching" — is one of the most underutilized options for people who can't afford full representation but need some legal expertise. Instead of hiring an attorney to handle your entire case, you hire one to handle just specific pieces of it.

Examples of what you might hire an attorney to do on an unbundled basis:

  • Review a settlement agreement or contract before you sign — $150–$400
  • Coach you on what to say at a hearing — $100–$250/hour for prep sessions
  • Draft a specific motion or letter that you file yourself — $200–$600
  • Appear at a single hearing on your behalf — $500–$1,500
  • Advise you on strategy — $150–$350/hour consultation

Not all attorneys offer limited scope representation, but increasingly many do as awareness of the access-to-justice gap grows. Ask directly: "Do you offer limited scope representation or legal coaching?" Many will say yes.

Option 7: Representing Yourself (Pro Se)

You have the right to represent yourself in virtually all civil court proceedings. This is called appearing "pro se" (Latin for "for oneself"). Courts must give pro se litigants some procedural latitude — though they're held to the same substantive legal standards as attorneys.

When self-representation works: Simple uncontested divorce, small claims cases, traffic tickets, straightforward landlord-tenant matters, and cases where you're simply seeking a single clear outcome (a fee dispute, an unpaid debt). Many courts have self-help centers with staff who can explain forms and procedures (not give legal advice, but help you understand the process).

When self-representation is risky: Complex family court matters with disputed custody, criminal defense, immigration proceedings, and cases with represented opposing parties who have strategic advantages. Even well-prepared pro se litigants often don't know what they don't know — procedural mistakes can result in case dismissal or losing on a technicality.

📊 Example: Combining Options for a Divorce

Maria is divorcing her husband and can't afford a full-representation attorney. Their main disagreement is how to split a retirement account. She uses a legal aid referral to find a local resource, downloads a free divorce petition from her state court's self-help center, pays a limited-scope attorney $400 to review her settlement agreement and advise on the QDRO process, and represents herself at the hearing. Total out-of-pocket: $400 plus court filing fee ($200). A full-representation attorney would have cost $3,000–$5,000 for the same matter.

A Word About Legal Insurance

If your situation isn't urgent, consider legal insurance for the future. Some employers offer legal insurance (similar to dental insurance) as an employee benefit through providers like LegalShield or MetLife Legal Plans. These typically cost $15–$30/month and cover a substantial amount of legal work — consultations, document review, simple legal matters, and discounts on more complex representation. If your employer offers it, it can be worth enrolling during open enrollment even if you don't have an immediate need.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is legal aid and who qualifies?

Legal aid organizations provide free civil legal services to people with low incomes, typically those earning below 125–200% of the federal poverty level. For 2026, that's roughly $19,000–$31,000/year for an individual, or $39,000–$63,000/year for a family of four. Legal aid handles cases involving housing, domestic violence, family law, immigration, public benefits, and consumer protection — not typically criminal defense.

Do I have a right to a free lawyer?

In criminal cases where you face potential jail time, yes — the Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to a public defender if you cannot afford an attorney. However, there is no constitutional right to a free attorney in civil cases (divorce, landlord-tenant, custody, debt collection). For civil matters, you must seek legal aid, pro bono services, or represent yourself.

What is unbundled legal services?

Unbundled legal services means an attorney handles only a specific part of your case — reviewing a document, coaching you on what to say at a hearing, or drafting one motion — rather than full representation. You pay for only what you need, which can dramatically reduce legal costs. Many attorneys who don't offer full representation for your matter may still offer unbundled services at hourly rates.

Can I represent myself in court?

Yes — representing yourself (called appearing "pro se") is your legal right in virtually all civil court proceedings. Courts are required to give some latitude to self-represented litigants, and many have self-help centers to assist with forms and procedures. Self-representation works best in straightforward cases like simple divorce, small claims, or uncontested matters.
