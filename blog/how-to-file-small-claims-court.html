How to File a Small Claims Court Case: A Plain-English Guide

Owed money? Damaged property? Small claims court is designed for regular people — no lawyer required.

📅 June 2026  ·  8 min read  ·  Small Claims
Written and researched by Brenda Kay  ·  Methodology & Sources

Small claims court is one of the most underused tools available to ordinary people. If someone owes you money, damaged your property, violated a contract, or kept your security deposit without justification, you can sue them yourself — no attorney needed — for relatively low filing fees. The process is designed to be accessible.

This guide walks you through every step, from deciding whether to file to collecting your judgment. Use our small claims calculator to estimate your filing fees and what you're likely to recover.

Is Small Claims Court Right for Your Situation?

Small claims court handles relatively straightforward civil disputes involving money. Common cases include:

  • Landlord keeping a security deposit without cause
  • Contractor who took money but didn't complete work
  • Car accident damage the other driver won't pay
  • Unpaid loans to friends or family
  • Defective products the seller won't refund
  • Unpaid invoices for services you provided
  • Property damaged by a neighbor (fallen tree, flood, etc.)

Small claims court does not handle: divorce, custody, felony crimes, injunctions, or cases where you want something other than money (like forcing someone to do something).

💡 Before you file, send a formal demand letter by certified mail. It's required by some courts, shows you tried to resolve it, and sometimes prompts payment without going to court. State your claim, the amount owed, and give a 10–14 day deadline.

Dollar Limits: How Much Can You Sue For?

Each state caps the amount you can claim in small claims court. Common limits in 2026:

  • California: $12,500 (individuals)
  • Texas: $20,000
  • New York: $10,000
  • Florida: $8,000
  • Illinois: $10,000
  • Georgia: $15,000
  • Washington: $10,000
  • Ohio: $6,000

If your damages exceed the limit, you have two choices: waive the excess and sue for the limit, or file in regular civil court (which is more complex and may benefit from an attorney). Most people find it better to take the guaranteed small claims route for disputes under the limit.

Step-by-Step: How to File Your Case

  1. Find the right court. File in the county where the defendant lives or does business, or where the dispute occurred. Look up your local small claims court at the county clerk's website or call the courthouse.
  2. Get the correct defendant information. You need the defendant's full legal name and current address. If suing a business, use the registered business name — check your state's Secretary of State website for LLCs and corporations.
  3. Complete the claim form. This is usually a one-page form called a "Plaintiff's Claim" or "Statement of Claim." Describe what happened in plain language, the amount you're owed, and why. Available at the courthouse or online.
  4. File and pay the filing fee. Fees range from $30 to $100 depending on your state and claim amount. File in person or online where available.
  5. Serve the defendant. The court usually handles this by certified mail, or you can arrange process service. The defendant must be properly notified before the court date.
  6. Prepare your evidence. This is where cases are won or lost.

How to Prepare and Win Your Hearing

Small claims hearings are informal, usually 10–20 minutes. The judge or magistrate will hear both sides and ask questions. Here's how to make your case effectively:

  • Organize your evidence in a binder: Contracts, receipts, text messages, emails, photos, invoices — anything that supports your story
  • Make three copies: One for you, one for the defendant, one for the judge
  • Prepare a clear, concise story: What happened, when, how much you're owed, and why. Practice saying it in under 3 minutes
  • Bring witnesses if possible: Anyone who saw what happened can testify
  • Stay calm and professional: Judges react poorly to emotional outbursts; stick to the facts

📊 Example: Security Deposit Case

Tenant vs. landlord for $1,800 wrongfully withheld deposit:
Evidence to bring: move-in/move-out photos, lease agreement, written move-out inspection, certified mail demand letter, any text communications about the deposit.

Filing fee: ~$75. Potential recovery: $1,800 deposit + court costs + sometimes 2x deposit as penalty for bad-faith withholding under state law.

After You Win: Collecting Your Judgment

Winning a small claims judgment doesn't automatically get you paid. If the defendant doesn't pay voluntarily, you'll need to enforce the judgment:

  • Wage garnishment: File with the court to have their employer deduct payments from their paycheck
  • Bank levy: If you know where they bank, you can levy their account
  • Property lien: Place a lien on real estate they own
  • Till tap: For businesses, arrange a one-time collection from their cash register

⚠️ A judgment is only worth something if the defendant has assets to collect from. Before you spend time and money suing someone, consider whether they're "judgment-proof" — broke, unemployed, or about to file bankruptcy. Winning a judgment you can't collect is a hollow victory.

Common Mistakes That Lose Small Claims Cases

Even when you have a valid claim, these mistakes can sink your case:

  • Suing the wrong party. If a business is an LLC or corporation, you must sue the entity, not the owner personally. Get the exact registered name from the Secretary of State's website.
  • Missing the statute of limitations. You only have a limited time to file after the dispute occurred — typically 2–6 years depending on your state and the type of claim. Miss it and your case is dismissed regardless of merit.
  • Not sending a demand letter first. Some courts require it; others see it as evidence of good faith. Always send one by certified mail before filing.
  • Showing up without organized evidence. Judges see dozens of cases. The plaintiff who hands up a neat binder with labeled tabs wins more often than the one waving a stack of crumpled receipts.
  • Asking for the wrong amount. Only claim what you can document. Inflated claims destroy your credibility. Be precise: $1,847.23 looks more credible than "about $2,000."
  • Suing someone who is judgment-proof. Winning feels good; collecting is what matters. If the defendant has no job, no bank account, and no property, a judgment is worthless paper.

What to Expect at the Hearing

Small claims hearings are informal but still a court proceeding. Here's what typically happens:

  1. Both parties are sworn in
  2. You (the plaintiff) present your case first — explain what happened, show your evidence
  3. The defendant responds and presents their side
  4. The judge may ask questions of both sides
  5. The judge issues a ruling — sometimes immediately, sometimes by mail within a few days

Keep your presentation under 3 minutes. State the facts chronologically. Let your documents do the heavy lifting. Don't interrupt the defendant or the judge. Address the judge as "Your Honor."

Frequently Asked Questions About Small Claims Court

What are the small claims court dollar limits by state?

Small claims court limits vary significantly by state. California: $12,500 for individuals; Texas: $20,000; New York: $10,000; Florida: $8,000; Illinois: $10,000; Georgia: $15,000; Pennsylvania: $12,000; Ohio: $6,000; Michigan: $7,000; Washington: $10,000; North Carolina: $10,000. A few states go as high as $25,000. Always verify at your local court's website — limits and fees change periodically.

How long does a small claims court case take?

After filing, your hearing is typically scheduled 30–70 days out. The hearing itself usually lasts 10–30 minutes. If you win and the defendant doesn't appeal, you can begin collection immediately. Getting paid can take additional months if the defendant resists — you may need wage garnishment or a bank levy. Total time from filing to payment is usually 2–6 months for cooperative defendants, 6–18 months for resistant ones.

Do I need a lawyer for small claims court?

Small claims court is designed for self-representation. Many states prohibit or limit attorneys at the hearing itself. You represent yourself — the judge guides the process. Focus on clear documentation: contracts, photos, receipts, texts, emails. A well-organized binder of evidence beats a disorganized case every time, even one with a lawyer present.

What happens if the defendant doesn't show up?

If the defendant was properly served and doesn't appear, you'll likely receive a default judgment in your favor for the amount you claimed. This is essentially an automatic win on the liability question. You still need to collect — the court doesn't do that for you. And be careful: if service was improper, the defendant can potentially vacate the default judgment later.

Can the defendant countersue me?

Yes. In most states, the defendant can file a counterclaim against you — either in the same small claims proceeding or in a separate case. If the counterclaim exceeds the small claims limit, it may move the case to a higher court. This is relatively uncommon in straightforward payment disputes but happens more often in contractor/client and landlord/tenant cases where both sides have grievances.

What if I lose my small claims case?

You can typically appeal a small claims decision to a higher court within 30 days. The appeal process varies by state — some allow a completely new trial (de novo), others review only legal errors. Appeals involve more formal procedures and often benefit from an attorney. If you lose and don't appeal, the defendant may be awarded their court costs against you. If you simply disagree with the outcome but have no legal grounds for appeal, moving on is often the practical choice.
