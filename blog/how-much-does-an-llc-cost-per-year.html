How Much Does an LLC Cost Per Year? The Real Ongoing Costs (2026)

Formation is just the beginning. Here's what it actually costs to keep your LLC in good standing every year — state fees, registered agents, taxes, and more.

📅 August 2026  ·  10 min read  ·  Business Law

Most articles about LLC costs focus on formation — the one-time state filing fee and attorney fees to get your LLC up and running. That's the easy part to find. What's harder to find is a clear picture of the ongoing annual costs you'll face every year to keep your LLC in good standing.

Those costs matter. Miss an annual report deadline and your LLC can be administratively dissolved by the state — meaning you lose the liability protection you paid to create. This guide covers every recurring LLC expense so you can budget accurately and never get caught off guard.

The Four Categories of Annual LLC Costs

Annual LLC costs fall into four main categories. The total for most small business owners ranges from $200 to $1,500 per year, with the biggest variable being your state's franchise tax or annual report fee.

1. State Annual Report / Franchise Tax Fees

Most states require LLCs to file an annual (or biennial) report with the state to remain in good standing. Some states call this a "franchise tax," others call it a "registered agent fee" or just an "annual report fee." Whatever the name, the requirement is the same: pay up or risk dissolution. Fees range from $0 (Ohio, Missouri) to $800 minimum (California, which charges all LLCs a minimum $800 franchise tax regardless of revenue).

2. Registered Agent Service

Every LLC must maintain a registered agent — a person or company with a physical address in the state, available during business hours to accept legal documents. You can serve as your own registered agent for free (if you have a physical address in the state), but many business owners pay a registered agent service ($50–$300/year) for privacy, reliability, and to avoid missing important legal notices when they're traveling or unavailable.

3. Business Licenses and Permits

Depending on your industry and location, you may need to renew local business licenses annually. Costs range from $25 for a basic city business license to $500+ for regulated industries (contractors, healthcare, real estate). These aren't LLC-specific costs — they apply to any business structure — but they're part of the annual compliance picture.

4. Tax Compliance Costs

Tax preparation for an LLC adds cost beyond what you'd pay as a simple wage employee. A Schedule C filing (for single-member LLCs taxed as sole proprietors) typically costs $150–$400 at a CPA. A partnership return (Form 1065 for multi-member LLCs) costs $400–$1,000. If your LLC is taxed as an S-corporation, add $500–$1,500 for the additional complexity of Form 1120-S.

California's $800 Minimum Franchise Tax: The Big Warning

California's LLC costs deserve special attention because they catch so many business owners off guard. Every LLC doing business in California — or formed in California — must pay a minimum annual franchise tax of $800, regardless of whether the LLC made any money. A brand-new LLC formed in January pays $800 that year. An LLC with $0 in revenue still pays $800.

LLCs with higher revenue pay more: $900 on revenue of $250,000–$499,999, $2,500 on $500K–$999,999, and $6,000 on $1M–$4.99M. Additionally, California charges an annual $20 "Statement of Information" fee every two years for most LLCs.

The practical implication: forming an LLC in California is significantly more expensive to maintain than in most other states. If your business operates nationally, some owners form in Wyoming or Delaware and then register as a foreign LLC in California — but this still requires paying California's fees, and adds complexity. For most small businesses operating primarily in California, simply forming in California is the simplest approach despite the cost.

Hidden Annual LLC Costs to Budget For

Beyond the mandatory state fees and registered agent, several other annual costs catch LLC owners by surprise:

Bookkeeping and Accounting

Even the simplest LLC should maintain separate financial records. At minimum, expect to pay $500–$2,000/year for a bookkeeper to reconcile accounts quarterly and prepare year-end records for your tax preparer. DIY accounting with software like QuickBooks ($360–$660/year) or Wave (free) reduces this cost but requires your time.

Business Banking

Many business checking accounts charge monthly maintenance fees of $12–$30 unless you maintain a minimum balance. Some banks like Relay, Mercury, or Bluevine offer free business checking — worth considering for cost-conscious LLC owners.

Business Insurance

General liability insurance for a typical LLC runs $500–$2,000/year for a small service business. Professional liability (errors and omissions) insurance adds $800–$3,000/year. These aren't legally required but are highly advisable — the liability protection of an LLC is not unlimited, and professional errors or accidents can pierce that protection without adequate insurance.

Domain, Website, and Software

A professional business presence — domain name ($12–$20/year), website hosting ($100–$500/year), email ($6–$12/month), and basic business software — adds $500–$2,000/year for a typical small business.

📊 Real Annual Cost Example: Texas LLC vs. California LLC

A solo freelance designer's LLC in Texas: state franchise tax $0 (revenue under threshold), registered agent service $99/year, business checking $0 (Relay free), QuickBooks $360/year, CPA for Schedule C $350/year. Total: ~$809/year. The same business in California: minimum franchise tax $800, registered agent $99, statement of information $10 (biennial average), QuickBooks $360, CPA $350 (higher for CA forms). Total: ~$1,619/year — double the Texas cost before a dollar of revenue.

When to Consider an S-Corp Election

One of the most powerful — and least understood — LLC cost-saving moves is electing S-corporation tax treatment once your LLC profits exceed roughly $40,000–$60,000 per year. Here's why it matters:

By default, all LLC profits pass through to your personal return and are subject to self-employment tax (15.3% on the first $168,600 in 2026). An LLC taxed as an S-corp lets you pay yourself a "reasonable salary" (subject to payroll taxes) and take additional profits as distributions not subject to self-employment tax. On $100,000 in profit, the SE tax savings can be $8,000–$12,000 per year — easily justifying the additional compliance costs of S-corp taxation.

The tradeoffs: S-corp taxation requires payroll (typically $50–$100/month for a payroll service), quarterly estimated tax deposits, and Form 1120-S ($500–$1,500 additional CPA fee). The math typically favors S-corp election when net profits consistently exceed $60,000–$80,000.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does an LLC cost per year to maintain?

Annual LLC maintenance costs typically range from $200 to $800 per year for most small business owners. This includes the state annual report or franchise tax fee ($50–$800 depending on state), a registered agent fee ($50–$300/year if using a service), and any state-specific LLC taxes. Federal taxes depend on how your LLC is taxed — as a sole proprietorship, partnership, or S-corp.

Do LLCs pay annual fees in every state?

Most states require LLCs to file an annual report and pay a fee to maintain good standing. The fees range from $0 (Ohio, Missouri) to $800+ (California's minimum franchise tax). Some states charge a flat fee; others charge based on revenue or number of members. Failure to file and pay can result in your LLC being dissolved by the state.

What is a registered agent and do I need one?

A registered agent is a person or company designated to receive official legal and government documents on behalf of your LLC — including lawsuits, IRS notices, and state compliance mail. Every LLC is required by law to maintain a registered agent with a physical address in the state where the LLC is formed. You can serve as your own registered agent (free) or hire a registered agent service ($50–$300/year) for privacy and convenience.

What taxes does an LLC pay?

By default, a single-member LLC is taxed as a sole proprietorship (profits reported on Schedule C of your personal return) and a multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership. Either can elect to be taxed as an S-corporation, which can reduce self-employment taxes once profits exceed roughly $40,000–$60,000 per year. Some states also impose a state-level LLC tax or franchise fee regardless of federal election.
