How Much Does a Divorce Really Cost in 2026?

Divorce costs in the United States vary more dramatically than almost any other legal expense — from as little as $300 for a simple online uncontested filing in a low-fee state to well over $100,000 per spouse in complex contested divorces with children, significant assets, and multiple attorneys. The single largest cost driver is whether the divorce is contested (spouses disagree on one or more issues) or uncontested (both agree on all terms). An uncontested divorce typically requires only court filing fees ($100–$435 depending on state) and possibly a small attorney or document preparation fee. A contested divorce requires attorney time for negotiations, hearings, and potentially trial.

The national average attorney hourly rate for family law cases reached $330/hour in 2026, according to the most recent ABA survey — but rates range from $175/hour in rural areas to $600–$800/hour in major metro areas like New York City, Los Angeles, and Chicago. A contested divorce can consume 30–100+ attorney hours on each side, explaining why total costs often reach $20,000–$50,000 per spouse when cases go to trial. The good news: only about 5% of divorces actually go to trial. The majority settle through negotiation or mediation at considerably lower cost.

The average total cost of divorce in the United States ranges from $7,000 to $30,000+ in 2026, depending on whether the divorce is contested or uncontested and the complexity of assets involved. Uncontested divorces — where both spouses agree on all terms — typically cost $500–$2,500 in filing fees and basic legal preparation. Contested divorces involving property disputes, child custody battles, or business assets can average $15,000–$30,000 per spouse, with complex cases running significantly higher.

What Drives Divorce Costs Up?

Attorney hourly rates are the primary variable — averaging $330/hour nationally in 2026, with California attorneys running $409/hour, New York at $426/hour, and Texas at $374/hour. When divorcing spouses can't agree on custody, property division, or support, those disagreements must be resolved through negotiation, mediation, or court hearings — each adding significant billable hours. A custody dispute alone typically adds $5,000–$20,000 to the total cost. High-asset divorces involving business valuation, real estate appraisal, or forensic accounting can reach $50,000–$100,000 or more when all professional fees are included. Geographic location also matters considerably: the same contested divorce that costs $20,000 in Ohio might run $60,000 in Manhattan simply because of the difference in attorney hourly rates and local court procedural requirements.

How to Reduce Your Divorce Costs

The most effective cost-reduction strategy is reaching agreements outside of court. Divorce mediation — using a neutral third party to help spouses reach decisions — typically costs $3,000–$8,000 total for both parties combined, far less than litigated divorces. In 2026, online uncontested divorce services offer flat-fee document preparation starting around $300–$500 plus court filing fees ($200–$450 depending on state), making simple uncontested divorces accessible for well under $1,000. Collaborative divorce, where both attorneys commit to avoiding court, is another cost-effective middle ground. If children or substantial assets are involved, even a few sessions with a mediator can prevent tens of thousands in future litigation costs. Before hiring attorneys, many couples find it productive to create a shared spreadsheet of assets and a draft parenting schedule — arriving at an attorney's office with an outline agreement in hand dramatically reduces billable hours.

What Are Typical Attorney Fee Structures for Divorce?

Most family law attorneys bill hourly and require an upfront retainer — typically $2,500–$10,000 for contested divorces. The retainer is applied toward billable hours; if the case resolves quickly, unused retainer funds are returned. Uncontested divorces are sometimes handled at flat fees of $500–$2,500. For low-income clients, legal aid organizations and law school family law clinics provide free or sliding-scale services. Court self-help centers in most states offer free forms and guidance for unrepresented parties filing uncontested divorces. Some courts, especially in California and Texas, have robust online divorce portals that walk you through the paperwork step-by-step.

Frequently Asked Questions

According to recent attorney surveys, the average total divorce cost in the U.S. in 2026 is approximately $12,900 — though this figure varies widely by state and case complexity. Uncontested divorces average $1,500–$3,500 when using an online service or limited-scope attorney, while fully contested divorces with trial can exceed $30,000 per spouse in most states.
Divorce attorneys in 2026 typically charge $250–$500 per hour in most states, with rates reaching $600–$800/hour in major metro areas like New York City, San Francisco, or Chicago. Most attorneys require a retainer of $2,500–$10,000 upfront, billed against as time is used. Flat-fee arrangements for uncontested divorces typically range from $1,500–$3,500 including filing fees.
An uncontested divorce — where both spouses agree on property division, debt allocation, and any child custody or support arrangements — is generally the least expensive option. Court filing fees for an uncontested divorce typically range from $100 in Wyoming to over $400 in California. Online divorce services charge $150–$500 for forms preparation, making total costs as low as $300–$900 in states with lower filing fees.
Court filing fees for divorce vary by state and county: California averages $435, Texas ranges from $250–$350, Florida averages $400–$410, New York averages $335, and lower-cost states like Mississippi charge around $100–$150. Most courts charge an additional fee of $50–$150 for the divorce summons or service of process. These fees are separate from any attorney costs.
Mediation is generally the most effective way to reduce divorce costs — a certified mediator typically charges $150–$300/hour and can help couples resolve disputes in 3–8 sessions, compared to months of contested litigation. Agreeing on child custody arrangements early, creating a full inventory of assets before meeting with an attorney, and using collaborative divorce processes can each reduce total legal fees by 30–60% on average.
The US uses two property division systems. Nine "community property" states (California, Texas, Arizona, Nevada, Washington, Idaho, Louisiana, New Mexico, Wisconsin) generally split marital property 50/50 between spouses. The remaining 41 states use "equitable distribution," where courts divide property "fairly" — which may not be 50/50 but considers factors like length of marriage, each spouse's income and earning capacity, contributions to the marriage, and economic circumstances. In either system, "separate property" (assets owned before marriage, inheritances, and gifts to one spouse) is typically not divided.
Most states have a mandatory waiting period before a divorce can be finalized, typically 30–90 days from the date the divorce petition is filed. Some states require a period of legal separation before filing: North Carolina requires 1 year of physical separation, and several other states have similar requirements. These waiting periods cannot be waived even in completely uncontested cases. The fastest divorces in the US are in states like Alaska, Nevada, and Idaho, where there are minimal waiting periods and simple uncontested cases can finalize in 3–4 months. California's mandatory 6-month period from service of process is one of the longest.
Alimony (also called spousal support or spousal maintenance) is not automatic — it's ordered when one spouse has a significantly lower income or earning capacity and the marriage was long enough to create economic dependence. Factors courts consider include length of marriage, income disparity, age and health of both spouses, the standard of living during marriage, and whether one spouse left work to raise children. Short marriages (under 5 years) rarely result in long-term alimony. Permanent alimony has largely been replaced by "rehabilitative" alimony (paid for a fixed period to allow the lower-earning spouse to gain skills and employment). In 2019, federal tax law changed: alimony is no longer tax-deductible for the payer or taxable income for the recipient for divorces finalized after December 31, 2018.
A legal separation creates a formal, court-ordered arrangement covering property division, support, and custody while keeping the marriage legally intact. Couples choose separation over divorce for reasons including religious beliefs, desire to maintain health insurance coverage through a spouse's employer plan, Social Security benefit preservation (some benefits require 10 years of marriage), or simply needing time before making a permanent decision. A separation can later be converted to a divorce in most states. Note: Not all states recognize legal separation as a distinct legal status. Costs are similar to divorce since the same legal issues must be resolved.

7 Ways to Reduce Your Divorce Costs

  1. Try mediation first. A single mediator for both spouses costs $3,000–$8,000 total — far less than two attorneys. Many issues can be resolved in 3–5 sessions.
  2. Agree on parenting before anything else. Custody disputes are the #1 cost driver. If you can agree on a parenting schedule early, attorney fees drop dramatically.
  3. Create a shared asset inventory. Walking into an attorney's office with a complete, agreed-upon list of marital assets and debts saves hours of discovery time.
  4. Use limited-scope representation. Many attorneys offer "unbundled" services — you pay them to advise you and review documents rather than manage the entire case. This can cut costs by 50–70% vs. full representation.
  5. File online for uncontested divorces. Services like It's Over Easy, Hello Divorce, and state court self-help portals offer guided document preparation for $300–$800 — appropriate when you and your spouse fully agree.
  6. Avoid reactive communication. Every angry email or text that prompts a legal response costs money. Communicate through a co-parenting app or directly where possible.
  7. Consider collaborative divorce. Both attorneys pledge to resolve the case without court. If it fails, both must withdraw — a strong incentive for everyone to reach agreement.

⚖️ When to Hire a Divorce Attorney

Hiring a family law attorney is strongly advisable when: minor children are involved and custody or support is disputed; you and your spouse have significant shared assets (home, retirement accounts, business interests); there is a significant income disparity between spouses that may affect spousal support; there is any history of domestic violence or financial abuse; or your spouse has already retained an attorney. Even for amicable divorces, a one-time consultation with a family law attorney — typically $200–$350 for an hour — can help you identify legal rights you may not know you have before signing any agreement.
