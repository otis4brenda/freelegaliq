Understanding USCIS Immigration Fees in 2026

USCIS (U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services) implemented its largest fee increase in agency history on April 1, 2024 — an average increase of approximately 26% across most form types. These fee levels remain in effect as of 2026. The increases were driven by the agency's need to fund operations without relying on Congressional appropriations, as USCIS is almost entirely fee-funded. For applicants, this means planning budgets carefully before filing any immigration application, since fees are generally non-refundable even if the application is denied.

Beyond the USCIS filing fees themselves, most immigration cases involve additional costs: biometric services fees (included in most current filing fees), immigration attorney fees ($1,500–$5,000 for most standard cases), medical examination costs (required for green card applicants), and for court-based cases, EOIR (immigration court) fees — which jumped dramatically to $1,670 in June 2026 from the prior $130 level. Immigration law is also one of the most frequently changing areas of US law; regulations, policies, and even the availability of certain programs can shift significantly with each administration.

Important 2026 update: On June 12, 2026, a federal judge struck down the $100,000 H-1B employer registration fee that had been proposed by the DOJ. The DOJ is appealing that ruling. Always verify current fee amounts at uscis.gov/fees before filing any application, as fees can change with relatively short notice.

Frequently Asked Questions

USCIS raised most filing fees substantially in April 2024 — an average increase of about 26% across form types, the largest fee hike in the agency's history. The I-485 adjustment of status fee rose to $1,440 for adults, the N-400 naturalization application increased to $760, and the I-130 family petition fee rose to $675. These fee levels remain in effect through 2026.
The total cost to become a U.S. citizen from a green card is typically $760–$2,000+: $760 for the N-400 application, with optional costs for citizenship prep classes ($100–$300) and attorney review ($500–$1,500). Attorney assistance generally ranges from $500 to $1,500 for standard naturalization cases, though complex cases may cost more.
Most USCIS fees are non-refundable, even if an application is denied or withdrawn. For high-cost applications like the I-485 ($1,440), it is generally advisable to verify eligibility, gather all required supporting documents, and have a licensed immigration attorney or accredited representative review the application before submitting.
Low-income applicants may qualify for a filing fee waiver using Form I-912. Eligibility is generally based on income below 150% of the federal poverty guideline, receipt of means-tested public benefits, or demonstrated financial hardship. Fee waivers are not available for all form types — check uscis.gov for the current list of waiver-eligible applications and income thresholds.
USCIS processing times vary widely by form type and service center workload. In 2026, typical ranges include 3–6 months for I-765 employment authorization, 8–14 months for N-400 naturalization, and 12–36+ months for I-485 adjustment of status depending on the applicant's visa category. Current processing time estimates are available at uscis.gov/tools/case-status-online.
Adjustment of status (AOS) using Form I-485 is for people who are already in the US on a valid visa and want to become permanent residents without leaving. Consular processing is for people who are outside the US — they apply through a US embassy or consulate in their home country. Both paths lead to a green card, but use different forms, fees, and timelines. AOS allows the applicant to remain in the US (and work, with an approved EAD) while the case is pending. Consular processing is often used when the person is outside the US or when AOS is not available due to visa overstay or entry without inspection.
The path from a green card to citizenship (naturalization) requires being a lawful permanent resident for 3 years (if married to a US citizen) or 5 years, demonstrating good moral character, and passing the English and civics tests. The N-400 filing fee is $760 (2026). With an immigration attorney, total costs typically run $2,500–$4,000. The civics test covers 100 questions about US history and government — free study materials are available at uscis.gov. Many libraries and community organizations also offer free citizenship prep classes. Fee waivers are available for eligible low-income applicants via Form I-912.
A Request for Evidence (RFE) is a notice from USCIS asking for additional documentation to support your case. RFEs are common — they're issued in approximately 20–30% of H-1B petitions and many family and employment-based green card cases. Responding to an RFE typically costs $500–$2,500 in additional attorney fees depending on complexity. Ignoring an RFE or responding inadequately results in denial. If you receive an RFE, contact an immigration attorney immediately if you're not already working with one — the deadline is typically 87 days and the quality of the response significantly affects the outcome.
To sponsor a family member for a green card, the US petitioner (sponsor) must file an Affidavit of Support (Form I-864) showing income of at least 125% of the federal poverty guideline for the household size (including the immigrant). In 2026, that's approximately $20,800/year for a household of 2, $26,200 for a household of 3. If income is insufficient, a joint sponsor can co-sign the I-864. The I-864 is a legally enforceable contract — if the immigrant receives certain public benefits, the sponsor can be sued for reimbursement by federal or state agencies. The obligation generally ends when the immigrant becomes a US citizen or has worked for 40 Social Security-qualifying quarters.

The US Immigration Process: What to Expect

Understanding the general flow of the US immigration process helps set realistic expectations for timeline and cost:

  1. Determine eligibility and category. Immigration benefits are strictly category-based: family (immediate relative vs. preference categories), employment-based (EB-1 through EB-5), diversity lottery, asylum/refugee, or special programs. Your category determines available forms, processing times, and whether there's a visa backlog.
  2. File the petition. Most cases start with a petition (I-130 for family, I-140 for employment) to establish the immigrant's eligibility category. This is separate from the green card application itself and typically takes 3–18 months to adjudicate.
  3. Wait for a visa number (if required). Immediate relatives of US citizens have no numerical cap and can proceed immediately. All other categories have annual numerical limits, creating backlogs that can last months to decades depending on the country of birth and category. Check the State Department's Visa Bulletin monthly for your priority date's status.
  4. File the green card application or schedule consular interview. Once a visa number is available, file I-485 (if in US) or proceed with NVC processing for consular interview. This stage includes biometrics, medical exam (I-693), and typically takes 1–2 years.
  5. Attend the interview (if required). Most family-based and adjustment of status cases include an in-person interview at a USCIS field office. Employment-based and some other categories may be interview-waived.
  6. Receive the green card and maintain status. Conditional green cards (for recent marriages) are valid for 2 years; standard green cards are valid for 10 years. Renewal costs $415 (I-90). After the required residency period, apply for naturalization (citizenship) to permanently secure your status.

⚖️ When to Hire an Immigration Attorney

You should strongly consider an immigration attorney in any of these situations: you have any prior immigration violations, visa overstays, or deportation orders; you have a criminal record, even for minor offenses (many misdemeanors trigger immigration consequences); your petition was denied or received a Request for Evidence (RFE); your employment-based case involves priority dates, portability, or layoffs; you are applying for benefits while in removal proceedings; your case involves a waiver (I-601, I-601A, I-212); or your green card application involves issues of inadmissibility. Free and low-cost immigration legal assistance is available through nonprofit accredited representatives — search at immigrationadvocates.org or your local legal aid office. Never use unauthorized notarios or immigration consultants, who cannot legally provide immigration legal advice.
