How Child Support Is Calculated in the US (2026)

Child support in the United States is determined by state-specific guidelines, not the discretion of individual judges. Most states use one of two primary models: the Income Shares Model (used by approximately 40 states), which considers both parents' gross incomes and estimates how much the couple would have spent on the child if still together, or the Percentage of Income Model, which applies a flat percentage to the paying parent's net income. Texas, Illinois, and New York use percentage-based formulas; California uses its own algorithmic "Guideline" calculation that accounts for custody time share and net disposable income.

Beyond the base amount, most child support orders include mandatory add-ons: health insurance premiums (the cost attributed to the child), unreimbursed medical expenses, and work-related childcare costs. These add-ons are typically split between parents proportionally to their incomes and can add 15–40% above the base calculation. The final order entered by the court is the only legally binding number — private agreements between parents are not enforceable unless incorporated into a court order.

How Child Support Is Calculated

In income shares states, the process is: (1) Add both parents' gross (or net, depending on state) monthly incomes; (2) Look up the basic child support obligation for that combined income and number of children in state guidelines tables; (3) Calculate each parent's percentage share of combined income; (4) The paying parent's share of the basic obligation is their base child support payment; (5) Adjustments are made for health insurance costs, childcare, extraordinary medical expenses, and custody time (with significant custody time often reducing the payment).

What Can Modify a Child Support Order?

Child support orders can be modified when there is a "substantial change in circumstances" — typically a change in income of 15–20% or more, a change in custody arrangement, a change in the child's needs, or one parent becoming unemployed or disabled. Either parent can petition the court for modification. Many states have automatic review processes every three years, particularly when the child receives public assistance. Important: private agreements between parents to informally pay less than the court order are not legally enforceable — you'll still owe the difference as arrears. Only a new court order changes the legal obligation.

What Does Child Support Actually Cover?

The base child support payment is intended to cover a child's basic needs: food, housing, clothing, and education expenses proportional to each parent's income. Most states build in additional "add-on" expenses on top of the base support: health insurance premiums, unreimbursed medical and dental costs, childcare expenses (daycare, after-school care), and sometimes extracurricular activities. These add-ons are typically split between parents in proportion to their respective incomes. It's important to understand that a child support order alone may not cover all of a child's actual expenses — high medical costs, private schooling, or significant childcare needs are often addressed separately in the court order.

Income imputation is when a court assigns income to a parent based on their earning capacity rather than their actual earnings. Courts use imputation when a parent is voluntarily unemployed or underemployed, has deliberately reduced their income to lower support, or has failed to seek employment consistent with their education and work history. Courts typically impute income based on recent earnings history, local job market data, and the parent's training and experience. A parent who quits a $100,000/year job to earn $30,000 may still have child support calculated on the higher figure if the court finds the income reduction was voluntary.

What to Do With Your Estimate

Your calculator result gives you a reasonable ballpark for planning purposes. Here's how to use it effectively:

  • If you're negotiating a separation agreement: Use the estimate as a baseline in conversations with your co-parent. Agreeing close to guideline amounts is far more likely to receive court approval quickly.
  • If you're preparing for court: Gather your pay stubs, W-2s, and last two years of tax returns. Courts require verified income documentation. Self-employed parents should prepare a profit and loss statement.
  • If you want a formal order: Contact your state's Child Support Services (CSS) office — they provide free enforcement, establishment, and modification services. This is the most cost-effective route if the other parent is cooperative.
  • If there's a dispute: Consult a family law attorney. The initial consultation (often $200–$350/hour) will help you understand your state's specific formula and what to expect.
  • If you believe the current order is wrong: File a modification petition. Document the change in circumstances carefully before filing. An attorney can help ensure the petition is filed correctly to maximize the chance of a favorable outcome.

⚖️ When to Hire a Family Law Attorney for Child Support

A family law attorney is advisable when: you and the other parent disagree on income figures or custody time that affects the calculation; one parent is self-employed (making income harder to verify); either parent has complex income including bonuses, commissions, or rental income; you are seeking a modification and expect the other party to contest it; or there are arrears disputes or enforcement issues. Child support services agencies in every state (DCSS, CSE, or similar) provide free enforcement and modification services for custodial parents without requiring private counsel — a useful first resource before spending on private legal fees.
