How Child Support Is Calculated

In income shares states, the process is: (1) Add both parents' gross (or net, depending on state) monthly incomes; (2) Look up the basic child support obligation for that combined income and number of children in state guidelines tables; (3) Calculate each parent's percentage share of combined income; (4) The paying parent's share of the basic obligation is their base child support payment; (5) Adjustments are made for health insurance costs, childcare, extraordinary medical expenses, and custody time (with significant custody time often reducing the payment).

What Can Modify a Child Support Order?

Child support orders can be modified when there is a "substantial change in circumstances" — typically a change in income of 15–20% or more, a change in custody arrangement, a change in the child's needs, or one parent becoming unemployed. Either parent can petition the court for modification. Many states have automatic review processes every few years. Note: private agreements between parents to pay less than the court order are not enforceable — only a new court order can change the obligation.

Frequently Asked Questions

In most states, child support is calculated using the Income Shares model, which considers both parents' gross monthly incomes, the number of children, and the parenting time split. The court determines a 'basic support obligation' from a statewide schedule, then allocates it proportionally based on each parent's share of combined income. For example, if one parent earns $4,000/month and the other earns $2,000/month, the higher earner typically contributes about two-thirds of the total support obligation. Six states use the simpler Percentage of Income model, which is based solely on the paying parent's income.
Nationally, average child support payments range from roughly $400 to $700 per month for one child, based on combined parental incomes in the $5,000–$8,000/month range. Higher-income households typically see obligations of $800–$1,500+ per month. These are general averages — actual amounts vary significantly by state, income levels, number of children, and whether the parties agree to deviate from guideline amounts for expenses like private school or extracurriculars.
Parenting time, or custody split, directly affects child support in most states. In Income Shares states, parents who have the children for more than 40%–50% of overnights typically receive a reduction in their support obligation, since they're directly covering more of the children's daily costs. Some states apply a 'shared custody adjustment' once the non-custodial parent exceeds a certain threshold of overnight visits. In Wisconsin and other Percentage-of-Income states, the child support obligation is a fixed percentage of the paying parent's income regardless of parenting time.
Child support orders can generally be modified when there has been a substantial change in circumstances — typically defined as a 15%–20% or more change in income, a significant change in custody arrangements, or major changes in the child's needs. Most states require the change to be ongoing, not temporary. Either parent can petition the court for a modification; the burden is on the requesting party to demonstrate the qualifying change. In some states, child support orders automatically adjust every 3 years through a review process.
The Income Shares model (used by 41 states) calculates support based on the combined income of both parents, reasoning that the child should receive the same proportion of parental income they would have received if the parents lived together. The Percentage of Income model (used by about 6 states, including Wisconsin) sets support as a fixed percentage of only the paying parent's income — for example, 17% for one child, 25% for two children. Income Shares tends to produce higher support amounts when the custodial parent earns significantly less than the non-custodial parent.
